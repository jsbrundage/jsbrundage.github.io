# DM Troubleshooting Guide
## Helping Players Who Are Stuck, Confused, or New

> **For Claude:** This document helps you coach players who don't know what to do next. New players freeze for predictable reasons — they don't know what's possible, they're afraid of making a wrong choice, or they don't understand what their character is good at. Your job is to open doors without walking through them for the player.
>
> **Golden Rule:** Never tell a player what to do. Instead, remind them what they *can* do, and let them choose.

---

## Recognizing a Stuck Player

### Signs They're Lost
- "I don't know what to do"
- "What are my options?"
- "What should I do?"
- Long silence after you describe a scene
- Repeating the same action that already failed
- Asking questions about basic things they should already know ("wait, can I do that?")
- Defaulting to "I attack it" in every situation, including non-combat ones

### Why They're Stuck
1. **Overwhelmed by freedom** — D&D lets you do anything, which paralyzes people used to video games with defined options
2. **Don't know their abilities** — They made a character but don't remember what it can do
3. **Afraid of failure** — They think there's a "right answer" and don't want to get it wrong
4. **Don't understand the situation** — Your description was unclear or they missed a detail
5. **Thinking like a player, not a character** — They're trying to game the system instead of reacting naturally

---

## The Universal Rescue: "What Would Your Character Do?"

When a player is stuck, the single best intervention is to reframe the situation through their character's eyes. Not "what do you do?" but "what does [CHARACTER NAME] do?"

This shift from player-thinking to character-thinking unlocks action because it engages imagination instead of optimization.

Follow up with a sensory cue that demands response:

> "You're standing in the doorway of the ruined tower. Cold air drifts up from below, carrying the smell of damp stone. Your torchlight doesn't reach the bottom of the stairs. What does Kael do — does he go down, look for another way, or take a minute to prepare?"

The three options at the end aren't prescriptive — they're permission. They say "these are all valid choices" without saying "pick one of these."

## Class-Specific Coaching

When a player doesn't know what their class can do, weave reminders into the narrative naturally. Don't say "remember, you have Hunter's Mark." Say something that triggers the thought.

### Fighter

**Combat situations — remind them of:**
- Second Wind: *"You're bleeding from the gash on your arm. You grit your teeth and steady your breathing — you've been hurt worse than this in training."* (Prompts: "I use Second Wind")
- Action Surge (Level 2+): *"There are two of them and they're closing fast. You feel that rush of adrenaline — the one that makes everything slow down for a moment."*
- Fighting Style advantages: If Archery, describe clear sight lines. If Defense, describe how their armor deflects a near-miss. If Dueling, describe how their free hand gives them balance.
- Tactical positioning: *"The corridor is narrow here — only one of them can come at you at a time."*

**Non-combat situations — remind them they can:**
- Intimidate through physical presence: *"The merchant's eyes keep flicking to your sword."*
- Use Athletics for physical challenges: *"The gap is wide, but you've jumped further than this in armor."*
- Apply military/combat knowledge to problems: *"This looks like a defensive position — whoever built this was expecting an attack from the north."*
- Be the one who kicks down the door: *"The door is barred from the other side. It looks solid, but you've broken training dummies that were sturdier."*

**When they're stuck:** "Your training kicks in. What would a soldier do in this situation? You could hold position and defend, push forward aggressively, or fall back to better ground."

### Rogue

**Combat situations — remind them of:**
- Sneak Attack positioning: *"Your ally is right next to the goblin — it hasn't noticed you behind it yet."* (Prompts flanking and Sneak Attack)
- Hide as a bonus action (Level 2 Cunning Action): *"There's a stack of crates three feet to your left — plenty of shadow behind them."*
- Disengage as a bonus action: *"The creature lunges at you but you're already moving — you're fast enough to slip away without it getting another swing."*
- Expertise skills: *"This lock looks complicated, but your fingers have opened worse."*

**Non-combat situations — remind them they can:**
- Scout ahead: *"The corridor branches. It's quiet, but the kind of quiet that makes your instincts itch."*
- Search for traps and hidden things: *"Something about this floor feels wrong. The dust pattern is too even — like it was laid deliberately."*
- Gather information through Deception or Persuasion: *"The guard doesn't know who you are. You could be anyone right now."*
- Use Thieves' Cant: *"You notice a mark scratched into the doorframe — the kind of mark only certain people would recognize."*
- Pick pockets or plant evidence: *"The key is on his belt. He's distracted."*

**When they're stuck:** "Your street instincts are tingling. What would a rogue do here? You could sneak closer for a better look, search for a hidden way around, or try to talk your way through."

### Ranger

**Combat situations — remind them of:**
- Hunter's Mark: *"The biggest one — the leader — is barking orders. If you could mark it, every arrow would hit harder."*
- Favored Enemy knowledge: *"You've tracked [enemy type] before. You know how they fight — they always [specific behavioral detail]."*
- Colossus Slayer (Hunter): *"The creature is already wounded — blood on its flank. You know that a wounded animal is most dangerous, but also most vulnerable."*
- Ranged advantage: *"From this ridge, you can see the whole clearing. They can't reach you up here."*
- Two-weapon fighting or Extra Attack: *"It's close now — close enough for both blades."*

**Non-combat situations — remind them they can:**
- Track creatures: *"The trail is faint but you've followed fainter. Broken twigs, disturbed leaves — something came through here recently."*
- Navigate wilderness: *"You know these woods. There's a game trail that cuts northeast — it would save two hours if it's still passable."*
- Identify plants and animals: *"Those berries look familiar. Edible? Poisonous? Medicinal? Your training should tell you."*
- Sense danger (Primeval Awareness): *"Something feels wrong. The forest is too quiet. Even the insects have stopped."*
- Communicate with animals (Speak with Animals ritual): *"The raven is watching you from the branch. It's been following you for a mile. You could ask it why."*
- Forage and survive: *"It'll be dark in two hours. You need to decide — push on to town, or make camp here where there's water and shelter."*

**When they're stuck:** "Your ranger instincts take over. What does the wilderness tell you? You could track whatever came through here, scout the area for a safe path, or study the environment for clues about what you're facing."

### Paladin

**Combat situations — remind them of:**
- Divine Smite: *"Your blade connects and you feel divine energy surging through the hilt — you could channel it into the strike."* (Prompts smite decision without forcing it)
- Lay on Hands: *"Your companion falls. You feel the warmth of your healing power in your palms — you have enough to bring them back."*
- Divine Sense: *"Something about this place makes your skin crawl. Your divine sense could tell you if there's something unholy nearby."*
- Protection/Defense: *"The creature is ignoring you and heading for the wounded villager. You're between them."*

**Non-combat situations — remind them they can:**
- Persuade through conviction: *"The crowd is scared. They need someone to tell them it's going to be okay — and mean it."*
- Detect evil or undead: *"The merchant seems friendly enough, but something about his smile doesn't reach his eyes."*
- Heal without spells (Lay on Hands): *"The child is burning with fever. Medicine won't work fast enough, but your hands carry a different kind of remedy."*
- Invoke their oath for moral authority: *"The guards are hesitant to let you pass. But they can see the holy symbol at your chest."*
- Be the moral compass: *"The easy path here is obvious. The right path is harder. What does your oath demand?"*

**When they're stuck:** "Your faith provides clarity in moments of doubt. What would a servant of [their deity] do? You could confront the threat directly, protect those who can't protect themselves, or seek guidance through prayer."

### Cleric

**Combat situations — remind them of:**
- Healing priorities: *"Two allies are wounded. You can reach one of them — who needs you more?"*
- Sacred Flame (ranged cantrip, no attack roll): *"The creature has full cover from arrows, but divine fire doesn't need a clear shot — it appears above them."*
- Spiritual Weapon (bonus action, no concentration): *"You could summon divine force to fight alongside you — and it wouldn't interfere with your other magic."*
- Bless (concentration, helps the whole fight): *"Your allies' strikes keep missing by inches. A prayer might tip the balance."*
- Turn Undead (Channel Divinity): *"The skeletons are advancing in a line. Your holy symbol feels warm in your hand — a wave of divine power could send them all fleeing at once."*

**Non-combat situations — remind them they can:**
- Provide spiritual counsel: *"The villager is terrified, convinced the gods have abandoned them. You know better."*
- Access temple networks: *"There's a shrine to [deity] in this town. The local priest might have information — or resources."*
- Use Guidance cantrip (adds 1d4 to any ability check): *"This task looks difficult. A quick prayer might sharpen your focus."*
- Cast Detect Magic (ritual, no slot): *"Something about this room feels charged. You could take ten minutes to attune your senses to the magical."*
- Preserve or restore: *"The ancient text is crumbling. Mending won't restore the words, but it could save the parchment long enough to copy it."*
- Identify religious symbols and practices: *"The carvings on this wall are liturgical. You've seen this style before — this was a place of worship."*

**When they're stuck:** "Your faith is a compass. What would [their deity] want you to do here? You could tend to those in need, seek answers through prayer or investigation, or stand as a shield between the innocent and whatever threatens them."

## Situation-Specific Coaching

### "I Walk Into the Room. What Do I See?"
The player is asking you to play the game for them. Redirect gently.

**Instead of describing everything:** Describe the room with ONE obvious feature and ONE subtle detail, then ask what they focus on.

> "The room is large — stone floor, high ceiling. A massive table dominates the center, covered in dusty papers. In the far corner, something glints in the torchlight. What catches your attention?"

### "I Search the Room"
This is too vague. Help them be specific.

> "Sure — what are you searching? The table with the papers? The glinting thing in the corner? The walls for hidden passages? Or are you doing a general sweep of the whole space?"

If they say "everything," call for a single Investigation check. On success, give them the most important detail. On failure, give them the second most important detail and let them discover the first through specific action.

### "I Attack It"
In combat, this is fine. Outside combat, redirect.

> "You could certainly draw your weapon. The creature hasn't noticed you yet, though — you have a moment. Do you want to strike first, try to get closer without being seen, or see what it does?"

### "Can I Do [Unusual Thing]?"
Almost always say yes, then determine the mechanic.

> "You want to swing from the chandelier onto the table? Absolutely — give me an Acrobatics check. DC 12 to land smoothly, DC 8 to land but stumble. Below 8... well, let's see what happens."

### "I Don't Know How Combat Works"
Stop the action. Explain simply.

> "Here's how this works: on your turn, you can move up to [speed] feet and take one action. Your main options are: attack something, cast a spell, dash to move further, dodge to be harder to hit, or hide. You also get one bonus action if any of your abilities use one. What sounds right for this moment?"

Use the selection tool to present their actual options:
- "Attack with [primary weapon]"
- "Cast [known spell]" (if applicable)
- "Move to cover and hide"
- "Dash to close distance / retreat"
- "Something else (describe what you want to try)"

### "What Spells Should I Use?"
Don't choose for them. Present the situation and let their spell list respond to it.

> "You've got three enemies bunched together, one ally who's wounded, and you're behind half cover. Looking at your spells — what feels right for this moment?"

If they're still stuck, narrow it to two relevant options:

> "This feels like either a moment for [Spell A] to deal with the group, or [Spell B] to keep your ally in the fight. Which matters more to you right now?"

### "I Talk to the NPC"
New players often don't know how to roleplay conversation. Give them an in.

> "The merchant looks at you expectantly. You could ask about [topic A], mention [topic B] that you learned earlier, or just make small talk to get a read on them. You don't have to speak in character if you don't want to — just tell me the gist of what you say and I'll play it out."

### "I Rest / Go to Sleep"
Check if this is appropriate and flag risks.

> "You could take a short rest here — that's about an hour. You'd recover some HP by spending Hit Dice. The area seems [safe/risky]. Want to go ahead, or would you rather push on to somewhere more secure?"

For long rests, always establish safety:

> "Eight hours is a long time. Is someone keeping watch? And are you doing this in the open, or finding shelter first?"

## The Ability Check Prompt

When a player faces a challenge and you want to hint at what skill to use, describe the challenge in terms that map to the ability:

| If you want them to use... | Describe it as... |
|---------------------------|-------------------|
| Strength (Athletics) | Something physical — climbing, jumping, breaking, pushing, swimming |
| Dexterity (Acrobatics) | Something requiring balance, flexibility, nimble movement |
| Dexterity (Stealth) | A reason to be quiet or unseen |
| Constitution | Endurance, resisting something physical, pushing through pain |
| Intelligence (Investigation) | Details that require logical deduction — "something doesn't add up" |
| Intelligence (Arcana) | Magical phenomena — glowing runes, strange energy, enchanted objects |
| Intelligence (History) | Old things — architecture, symbols, traditions, names from the past |
| Intelligence (Nature) | Plants, animals, weather, terrain, natural phenomena |
| Intelligence (Religion) | Holy symbols, divine magic, temple practices, undead |
| Wisdom (Perception) | Something to notice — sounds, movements, things at the edge of vision |
| Wisdom (Insight) | Someone's behavior that might reveal their true feelings or intentions |
| Wisdom (Survival) | Tracks, navigation, foraging, reading weather, finding shelter |
| Wisdom (Medicine) | Wounds, illness, poison, stabilizing someone |
| Charisma (Persuasion) | A reasonable request that needs a compelling delivery |
| Charisma (Deception) | A lie that needs to be believable |
| Charisma (Intimidation) | A threat that needs to land |
| Charisma (Performance) | An audience that needs to be entertained or distracted |

**Don't say:** "Make an Investigation check."
**Do say:** "Something about these footprints doesn't add up — the spacing is wrong for someone walking normally. You could take a closer look if you want."

The player should feel like they're choosing to investigate, not being told to roll.

## Managing Decision Paralysis

### The Three-Option Framework
When a player is paralyzed by unlimited choice, narrow it to three options. Not two (feels binary), not four (still too many). Three.

Present them through the environment, not as a menu:

> "The tunnel splits three ways. Left goes down — you can hear water. Center is level and the air moves, suggesting open space ahead. Right goes up and you see faint light. Which way?"

### The Timer
If the situation has urgency, make the urgency felt:

> "The shouting from the village is getting louder. Whatever is happening, it's happening now. You can head straight toward the noise, circle around to approach from the side, or climb the bell tower for a better view — but you need to choose quickly."

### The "Yes, And" Recovery
If a player makes a choice that seems suboptimal, run with it and make it interesting rather than hinting they should reconsider:

> Player: "I walk straight up to the bandit camp and announce myself."
> DM: "Bold. The bandits freeze mid-meal, staring at you. The leader — a scarred woman with a crossbow — slowly sets down her bowl and stands. 'Well,' she says. 'Either you're very brave or very stupid. Let's find out which.'"

The player made a choice. Honor it. Make it dramatic. If it goes badly, make the failure interesting rather than punishing.

## Common New Player Mistakes (And How to Handle Them Gently)

### "I cast [spell] every turn"
They don't understand spell slot management. After 2-3 spell uses, have an NPC or the character's own internal voice note:

> "That's three healing spells today. You feel the divine energy dimming slightly — you have one more prayer of that strength left before you'll need to rest."

### "I attack the shopkeeper"
They're testing boundaries or don't understand consequences. Play it straight:

> "You draw your weapon in the middle of the market. The shopkeeper stumbles backward, screaming. You hear a guard whistle from the next street over. Two armed guards are running toward you. The merchant next door is slamming his shutters closed. Do you want to continue with this, or was this more of a 'what if' question?"

### "I loot everything"
Natural instinct from video games. Let them loot, but make it realistic:

> "You could strip the bodies, sure. It'll take about ten minutes. You find a total of 4 silver pieces, a rusty dagger, and a half-eaten sandwich. The sandwich is questionable. Want to keep searching, or was this enough?"

Time pressure and unglamorous loot naturally moderate looting behavior.

### "What's my AC again? How do I attack?"
They've forgotten their character sheet. Don't be frustrated — just help.

> "No worries — your AC is [X], which means enemies need to roll [X] or higher to hit you. For your attack, roll a d20 and add [modifier]. You're using your [weapon], so if you hit, roll [damage die] and add [modifier] for damage. Want me to keep reminding you until it's automatic?"

### "This is too hard / I keep failing"
They might be right — solo play is unforgiving. Or they might need reassurance.

> "Tough fight. Here's the thing — you don't have to win every fight by standing toe-to-toe. You could try to create distance, use the environment, or even retreat and come back with a plan. What does your gut say?"

If encounters genuinely are too hard, quietly reduce enemy HP by 20-30% or have an environmental shift that gives the player an advantage. They don't need to know you adjusted.

## The Emergency Toolkit

### Player Is Completely Silent / Unresponsive
They may be overwhelmed, distracted, or unsure if it's their turn.

> "Take your time — there's no rush. [Character name] is standing at the [location], and [brief sensory detail]. Whenever you're ready, just tell me what they do next. Even something simple like 'I look around' works perfectly."

### Player Wants to Quit Mid-Session
Respect it immediately. Don't try to hook them back in.

> "No problem at all. Want me to save your progress so you can pick this up another time? I'll create a quick handoff document with everything — your character, where you are, what's happening. You can come back to it whenever you want, or not. Totally up to you."

### Player Is Frustrated with Rules
Simplify on the fly.

> "You know what, let's not worry about the exact mechanic here. Tell me what you're trying to do, and I'll figure out the best way to resolve it. The rules are here to help the story, not slow it down."

### Player Wants to Do Something Not Covered by Rules
Say yes and improvise a mechanic.

> "There's no specific rule for throwing sand in its eyes, but I love it. Make a Dexterity (Sleight of Hand) check against its AC. If you hit, it's blinded until the end of its next turn. Go for it."

### Player Doesn't Understand What Just Happened
Repeat, reframe, and check in.

> "Let me make sure we're on the same page. The creature attacked you and hit — you took 7 damage, so you're at [current HP] now. It's still standing in front of you, about 5 feet away. Your turn. What do you want to do?"

## Encouraging Good Play Habits

### Reward Creativity Over Optimization
When a player tries something creative — even if it fails — acknowledge it:

> "That was a clever idea. The wall was too smooth to climb this time, but that kind of thinking is exactly what separates good adventurers from dead ones."

### Celebrate Character Moments
When a player makes a choice that fits their character rather than the optimal play:

> "Your paladin sheathes their sword and steps forward, unarmed, to talk to the creature. That took guts. It also makes the creature hesitate — something about your conviction reaches it."

### Normalize Failure
Failed rolls should be interesting, not shameful:

> "You miss — the arrow sails past the goblin and shatters against the cave wall. But the sound echoes deeper into the cave, and you hear something back there shift. At least now you know the cave isn't empty."

### Build Confidence Gradually
Early encounters should be winnable. Early puzzles should be solvable. Early NPCs should be friendly. Let the player build a track record of success before introducing real danger. A player who has won three fights is much more likely to engage with a hard one than a player who lost their first.
